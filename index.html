Section 01 • Asymptotic Probability Theory

Modes of Convergence in Probability Theory

Unlike deterministic sequences of real numbers $(x_n)_{n \ge 1}$, a sequence of random variables $(X_n)_{n \ge 1}$ defined on a probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ consists of measurable functions $X_n: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$. Because randomness can be quantified in several distinct ways—pointwise on $\Omega$, in probability measure, via moments, or solely through cumulative distribution functions—probability theory defines four primary modes of convergence.

Strongest Pointwise Mode $X_n \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} X$

1. Almost Sure Convergence (a.s. / w.p. 1)

The sequence $X_n(\omega)$ converges pointwise to $X(\omega)$ for every outcome $\omega \in \Omega$, except possibly on a null set $N \in \mathcal{F}$ with $\mathbb{P}(N) = 0$.

$$\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\omega \in \Omega : \lim_{n \to \infty} X_n(\omega) = X(\omega)\right\}\right) = 1$$
Equivalent characterization: $\forall \varepsilon > 0,\; \lim_{n\to\infty} \mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{k \ge n} |X_k - X| > \varepsilon\right) = 0$
Intuition: Look at a single entire sample path $\omega$ across all time $n = 1, 2, \dots$. With probability 1, the trajectory eventually enters and never leaves any $\varepsilon$-neighborhood of $X(\omega)$.
Moment / Hilbert Space Mode $X_n \xrightarrow{L^2} X$

2. Mean-Square Convergence ($L^2$ / Quadratic Mean)

Assuming $\mathbb{E}[X_n^2] < \infty$ and $\mathbb{E}[X^2] < \infty$, the expected squared distance between $X_n$ and $X$ vanishes as $n \to \infty$. More generally, $L^r$ convergence requires $\mathbb{E}[|X_n - X|^r] \to 0$.

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} \mathbb{E}\left[(X_n - X)^2\right] = 0 \iff \text{Bias}(X_n)^2 + \text{Var}(X_n - X) \to 0$$
Requires existence of finite second moments ($X_n, X \in L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$)
Intuition: Heavily penalizes large deviations (outliers) due to squaring. In statistics, $L^2$ convergence of an estimator $\hat{\theta}_n \to \theta$ is identical to Mean Squared Error ($\text{MSE}$) tending to zero.
Consistency Standard $X_n \xrightarrow{P} X$

3. Convergence in Probability

For any arbitrarily small tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$, the probability that $X_n$ deviates from $X$ by more than $\varepsilon$ approaches zero as $n$ increases.

$$\forall \varepsilon > 0, \quad \lim_{n \to \infty} \mathbb{P}\left(|X_n - X| > \varepsilon\right) = 0$$
Defines weak consistency of statistical estimators: $\hat{\theta}_n \xrightarrow{P} \theta$
Intuition: At a large fixed snapshot $n$, $X_n$ is very likely close to $X$. However, rare "spikes" may still recur infinitely often across $n$, so individual sample paths need not settle down forever.
Weak Convergence of Laws $X_n \xrightarrow{d} X$

4. Convergence in Distribution (Weak Convergence)

The cumulative distribution functions $F_{X_n}(x) = \mathbb{P}(X_n \le x)$ converge to $F_X(x) = \mathbb{P}(X \le x)$ at every continuity point $x$ of $F_X$.

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} F_{X_n}(x) = F_X(x) \quad \forall x \in C(F_X)$$
Equivalent (Lévy's Continuity Theorem): $\varphi_{X_n}(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{itX_n}] \to \varphi_X(t)\;\forall t \in \mathbb{R}$
Intuition: $X_n$ and $X$ do not even need to live on the same probability space! Only their probability laws (histograms/CDFs) look increasingly identical as $n \to \infty$.

1. Why $L^2 \implies P$ (Chebyshev)

By Markov's inequality applied to $(X_n - X)^2$: $$\mathbb{P}(|X_n - X| > \varepsilon) = \mathbb{P}\big((X_n - X)^2 > \varepsilon^2\big) \le \frac{\mathbb{E}[(X_n - X)^2]}{\varepsilon^2} \to 0.$$ Similarly, $L^r \implies L^s$ for all $r > s \ge 1$ via Lyapunov's / Jensen's inequality.

2. Why $P \not\implies \text{a.s.}$ (Typewriter Sequence)

On $\Omega = [0,1]$, define sliding indicator intervals $I_{k,j} = [(j-1)/2^k, j/2^k]$ of shrinking width $2^{-k}$. Then $\mathbb{P}(X_n = 1) = 2^{-k} \to 0$, so $X_n \xrightarrow{P} 0$. Yet for every $\omega \in [0,1]$, $X_n(\omega) = 1$ infinitely often, so $\limsup X_n(\omega) = 1 \ne 0$. (However, a subsequence $X_{n_k} \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} 0$ always exists!)

3. Why $\text{a.s.} \not\implies L^2$ (Shrinking High Spike)

Let $X_n = n$ with probability $1/n^2$ and $0$ otherwise. By Borel–Cantelli ($\sum 1/n^2 < \infty$), $X_n \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} 0$ and $X_n \xrightarrow{P} 0$. However, $\mathbb{E}[X_n^2] = n^2 \cdot \frac{1}{n^2} = 1 \not\to 0$. Reverse ($L^2 \implies \text{a.s.}$) holds if $\sum \mathbb{E}[(X_n - X)^2] < \infty$, and $P \implies L^2$ holds under Uniform Integrability (Vitali's Theorem).

Roles in the Law of Large Numbers, Central Limit Theorem & Core Statistical Theorems

Let $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n$ be i.i.d. random variables with finite mean $\mu = \mathbb{E}[X_1]$ and sample mean $\bar{X}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i$.

Theorem / Statistical Result Convergence Mode Mathematical Statement & Conditions Statistical Significance
Weak Law of Large Numbers (WLLN) $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{P} \mu$ Requires $\mathbb{E}[|X_1|] < \infty$ (Khinchin's WLLN). If $\sigma^2 < \infty$, proved directly via Chebyshev: $\mathbb{P}(|\bar{X}_n - \mu| > \varepsilon) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2} \to 0$. Establishes weak consistency of sample averages and relative frequencies in large samples.
Strong Law of Large Numbers (SLLN) $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} \mu$ Kolmogorov's SLLN: $\mathbb{P}(\lim_{n\to\infty} \bar{X}_n = \mu) = 1 \iff \mathbb{E}[|X_1|] < \infty$. Guarantees strong consistency: a single Monte Carlo simulation run converges to $\mu$ with probability 1.
$L^2$ Law of Large Numbers $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{L^2} \mu$ When $\sigma^2 = \text{Var}(X_1) < \infty$, $\mathbb{E}[(\bar{X}_n - \mu)^2] = \text{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \to 0$. Provides explicit $O(1/n)$ MSE decay rate and underpins Hilbert-space projection theory in regression.
Central Limit Theorem (CLT) $\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ Lindeberg–Lévy CLT: requires i.i.d. with $0 < \sigma^2 < \infty$. Equivalently, $\frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. Enables asymptotic confidence intervals, $z$-/$t$-tests, and Gaussian approximations regardless of the parent distribution.
Slutsky's Theorem & CMT $\xrightarrow{d}$ & $\xrightarrow{P}$ If $X_n \xrightarrow{d} X$ and $Y_n \xrightarrow{P} c$, then $X_n + Y_n \xrightarrow{d} X + c$ and $X_n Y_n \xrightarrow{d} cX$. By Continuous Mapping Theorem (CMT), $g(X_n) \xrightarrow{d} g(X)$ for continuous $g$. Allows replacing unknown $\sigma$ with consistent sample SD $S_n \xrightarrow{P} \sigma$ in CLT: $\frac{\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu)}{S_n} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1)$.
Delta Method & Glivenko–Cantelli $\xrightarrow{d}$ & $\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}$ Delta Method: $\sqrt{n}(g(\bar{X}_n) - g(\mu)) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, [g'(\mu)]^2\sigma^2)$.
Glivenko–Cantelli: $\sup_{x \in \mathbb{R}} |\hat{F}_n(x) - F(x)| \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} 0$. 		Delta method derives asymptotic variance of nonlinear estimators (e.g. odds ratios); Glivenko–Cantelli is the "Fundamental Theorem of Statistics."
Section 02 • Scaling & Variance Stabilization

Why Does $\sqrt{n}$ Frequently Appear in Convergence Theorems?

The ubiquitous factor $\sqrt{n}$ (or $n^{-1/2}$) is not an arbitrary convention—it is a direct mathematical consequence of how variance scales under the addition of independent random variables.

01

Additivity of Variance Under Independence

For any random variables $X_1, \dots, X_n$ with finite variance, the variance of their sum $S_n = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i$ is:

$$\text{Var}(S_n) = \sum_{i=1}^n \text{Var}(X_i) + 2 \sum_{1 \le i < j \le n} \text{Cov}(X_i, X_j)$$

When $X_1, \dots, X_n$ are independent (or merely uncorrelated) with common variance $\sigma^2$, all $\binom{n}{2}$ covariance terms vanish. Thus, variance grows linearly with $n$: $$\text{Var}(S_n) = n\sigma^2 \implies \text{SD}(S_n) = \sqrt{\text{Var}(S_n)} = \sqrt{n}\,\sigma.$$

02

Collapse ($1/n$) vs. Stabilization ($1/\sqrt{n}$)

Dividing $S_n - n\mu$ by different powers $n^\alpha$ produces three distinct asymptotic regimes:

  • $\alpha = 1$ (LLN Regime): $\bar{X}_n - \mu = \frac{S_n - n\mu}{n}$ has variance $\frac{n\sigma^2}{n^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \to 0$. The distribution collapses to a degenerate point mass at $0$.
  • $\alpha = 1/2$ (CLT Regime): $\frac{S_n - n\mu}{\sqrt{n}} = \sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu)$ has constant variance: $$\text{Var}\big(\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu)\big) = n \cdot \frac{\sigma^2}{n} = \sigma^2.$$ It neither blows up ($\alpha < 1/2$) nor degenerates ($\alpha > 1/2$), yielding a non-trivial $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ limit.
03

Role of Independence & Statistical Inference

In statistical inference, $\sqrt{n}$-scaling dictates precision and uncertainty quantification:

  • Standard Error & $O_p(n^{-1/2})$ Rate: The typical estimation error $|\bar{X}_n - \mu|$ shrinks at rate $1/\sqrt{n}$. To cut confidence interval width in half, one needs $4\times$ the sample size.
  • Asymptotic Normality of MLEs: Because the log-likelihood $\ell_n(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log f(X_i; \theta)$ is a sum of $n$ independent score contributions, the MLE satisfies $\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}_{\text{MLE}} - \theta_0) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \mathcal{I}(\theta_0)^{-1})$.
  • When Independence Fails: If observations are positively correlated ($\text{Cov}(X_i, X_j) = \rho\sigma^2 > 0$), $\text{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}[1 + (n-1)\rho] \to \rho\sigma^2 > 0$—even LLN fails! In long-range dependent processes, scaling shifts to $n^H$ ($H \ne 1/2$).

Section 03 • Bayesian Probability & Statistical Inference

Conditional Probability, Bayes' Theorem & Statistical Inference

While frequentist inference treats unknown parameters $\theta$ as fixed constants and evaluates procedures across hypothetical repeated samples, Bayesian inference uses probability directly to quantify epistemic uncertainty about $\theta$, updating beliefs logically as new data $x$ arrive.

1. Conditional Probability in Probability & Statistics

In Kolmogorov's axiomatic probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$, given an event $B \in \mathcal{F}$ with $\mathbb{P}(B) > 0$, the conditional probability of event $A$ given $B$ restricts the sample space from $\Omega$ to $B$ and renormalizes total probability to $1$:

$$\mathbb{P}(A \mid B) = \frac{\mathbb{P}(A \cap B)}{\mathbb{P}(B)}, \qquad \mathbb{P}(B) > 0$$

Bridge to Statistics: In probability theory, we reason forward from a known model $\theta$ to data $X$ via the sampling distribution $p(x \mid \theta)$. In statistical inference, we observe data $X = x$ and must invert this conditioning to reason backward about the unknown state of nature $\theta$ via $p(\theta \mid x)$.

2. Bayes' Theorem (Discrete & Continuous Forms)

By symmetry of intersection $\mathbb{P}(A \cap B) = \mathbb{P}(B \mid A)\mathbb{P}(A) = \mathbb{P}(A \mid B)\mathbb{P}(B)$ and the Law of Total Probability, we obtain Bayes' Theorem. For a continuous parameter $\theta \in \Theta$ and observed data $x$:

$$p(\theta \mid x) = \frac{p(x \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)}{p(x)} = \frac{p(x \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)}{\displaystyle\int_{\Theta} p(x \mid \tilde{\theta})\,p(\tilde{\theta})\,d\tilde{\theta}}$$

Because the denominator $p(x)$ does not depend on $\theta$, Bayes' rule is often written in proportional form: $$p(\theta \mid x) \propto L(\theta; x) \cdot p(\theta)$$ "Posterior is proportional to Likelihood times Prior."

The 3 Fundamental Components of Bayesian Inference (+ Marginal Evidence)

Every Bayesian model links three core mathematical objects through Bayes' Theorem to transform pre-experimental knowledge into post-experimental knowledge.

1. PRIOR PROBABILITY $p(\theta)$

Pre-Data Belief Distribution

Quantifies uncertainty and domain knowledge about the parameter $\theta$ before observing the current dataset $x$.

  • Informative priors: Encode historical studies, physical constraints, or expert judgment.
  • Weakly informative / non-informative priors: E.g., Jeffreys' prior $p(\theta) \propto \sqrt{\det \mathcal{I}(\theta)}$ or flat priors when minimal prior bias is desired.
  • Conjugate priors: Chosen so $p(\theta \mid x)$ belongs to the same parametric family as $p(\theta)$.
2. LIKELIHOOD FUNCTION $L(\theta; x) = p(x \mid \theta)$

Data-Generating Mechanism

Evaluates the joint density/mass of the observed sample $x = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$ viewed as a function of $\theta$ for fixed data $x$: $$L(\theta; x) = \prod_{i=1}^n p(x_i \mid \theta) \quad (\text{for i.i.d. data}).$$

  • Note: $L(\theta; x)$ is not a probability density over $\theta$ (its integral over $\theta$ need not equal 1).
  • Serves as the channel through which empirical evidence modifies prior weights, obeying the Likelihood Principle.
3. POSTERIOR PROBABILITY $p(\theta \mid x)$

Updated Belief Distribution

The complete conditional distribution of $\theta$ given observed data $x$, obtained by multiplying $p(\theta)$ by $L(\theta; x)$ and normalizing by the marginal likelihood (evidence) $p(x) = \int p(x \mid \theta)p(\theta)\,d\theta$.

  • Yields full uncertainty quantification: posterior mean $\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid x]$, MAP mode, and direct Credible Intervals $\mathbb{P}(a \le \theta \le b \mid x) = 0.95$.
  • Enables Posterior Predictive forecasts: $p(x_{\text{new}} \mid x) = \int p(x_{\text{new}} \mid \theta)p(\theta \mid x)\,d\theta$.

Why Sequential Bayesian Updating Plays a Central Role in Inference

Suppose data arrive in two independent batches $x_1$ and $x_2$. Updating sequentially—using the posterior after $x_1$ as the new prior for $x_2$—produces the exact same posterior as pooling all data at once:

$$p(\theta \mid x_1, x_2) \propto p(x_2 \mid \theta)\,\underbrace{p(x_1 \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)}_{\propto\; p(\theta \mid x_1)} \propto p(x_2 \mid \theta)\,p(\theta \mid x_1)$$

Today's posterior is tomorrow's prior. This recursive coherence makes Bayesian inference ideal for online learning, adaptive clinical trials, Kalman/particle filtering, and scientific accumulation of knowledge. Furthermore, under regularity conditions, the Bernstein–von Mises Theorem guarantees that as $n \to \infty$, the likelihood overwhelms any smooth non-zero prior and the posterior converges to $\mathcal{N}(\hat{\theta}_{\text{MLE}}, [n\mathcal{I}(\theta_0)]^{-1})$, bridging Bayesian and frequentist asymptotics.

WORKED EXAMPLE 01 • DISCRETE Medical Screening & Base-Rate Effect

Diagnostic Testing ($D \in \{0,1\}$)

Consider a rare disease with prevalence (Prior) $\mathbb{P}(D=1) = 0.005$ ($0.5\%$). A diagnostic test has sensitivity (Likelihood under $D=1$) $\mathbb{P}(T=+ \mid D=1) = 0.98$ and specificity $\mathbb{P}(T=- \mid D=0) = 0.95$ (so false positive rate $\mathbb{P}(T=+ \mid D=0) = 0.05$).

1. Marginal Evidence $\mathbb{P}(T=+)$:
$$\mathbb{P}(T=+) = (0.98)(0.005) + (0.05)(0.995) = 0.0049 + 0.04975 = 0.05465$$
2. Posterior Probability $\mathbb{P}(D=1 \mid T=+)$:
$$\mathbb{P}(D=1 \mid T=+) = \frac{0.0049}{0.05465} \approx 0.0897 \quad (8.97\%)$$

Takeaway: Even with a $98\%$ sensitive test, a single positive result raises disease probability from $0.5\%$ to only $8.97\%$ because false positives from the large healthy population ($99.5\%$) outnumber true positives. If a second independent test is positive, using $0.0897$ as the new prior updates the posterior to $65.9\%$.

WORKED EXAMPLE 02 • CONTINUOUS Beta-Binomial Conjugate Updating

Estimating an Unknown Proportion $\theta \in (0,1)$

Suppose we observe $k$ successes in $n$ Bernoulli trials, giving Binomial likelihood $L(\theta; k, n) \propto \theta^k (1-\theta)^{n-k}$. Placing a conjugate $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ prior $p(\theta) \propto \theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1}$ yields:

$$p(\theta \mid k, n) \propto \theta^{\alpha + k - 1}(1-\theta)^{\beta + n - k - 1} \implies \theta \mid k, n \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha + k,\, \beta + n - k)$$
Posterior Mean as a Precision-Weighted Average:
$$\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid k, n] = \frac{\alpha + k}{\alpha + \beta + n} = \underbrace{\left(\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\alpha+\beta+n}\right)}_{w_{\text{prior}}} \underbrace{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}}_{\text{Prior Mean}} + \underbrace{\left(\frac{n}{\alpha+\beta+n}\right)}_{1 - w_{\text{prior}}} \underbrace{\frac{k}{n}}_{\text{MLE}}$$

Takeaway: $\alpha$ and $\beta$ act as "pseudo-counts" of prior successes and failures. For small $n$, the prior regularizes extreme empirical proportions (e.g. $k=0$ out of $n=2$ does not collapse to $\hat{\theta}=0$). As $n \to \infty$, $w_{\text{prior}} \to 0$ and the posterior concentrates around the MLE $k/n$.

Applications, Advantages, Limitations & Influence on Conclusions

Key Real-World Applications

  • A/B Testing & Bandits: Thompson sampling uses Beta-Binomial or Normal-Normal posteriors to balance exploration and exploitation in real time.
  • Clinical Trials & Epidemiology: Adaptive trial stopping rules and hierarchical disease mapping across regions with small sample sizes.
  • Hierarchical / Multilevel Models: Partial pooling across groups (e.g. schools, patients) shrinks noisy group estimates toward the population mean.
  • Bayesian Machine Learning & Kalman Filters: State-space tracking, spam filtering, and uncertainty-aware neural networks.

Advantages of Bayesian Inference

  • Direct Probabilistic Statements: Credible intervals directly state $\mathbb{P}(\theta \in [a,b] \mid x) = 0.95$, unlike frequentist confidence intervals which refer to long-run coverage.
  • Regularization & Small-Sample Stability: Informative priors prevent overfitting when $n$ is small or parameters are weakly identified.
  • Exact Finite-Sample Inference: Does not rely on asymptotic normality approximations when $n$ is small.
  • Natural Nuisance Parameter Integration: Integrates out nuisance parameters $\eta$ via $p(\theta \mid x) = \int p(\theta, \eta \mid x)\,d\eta$.

Limitations & Influence on Conclusions

  • Prior Sensitivity: When $n$ is small or data are sparse, different priors can materially alter posterior conclusions; prior sensitivity analysis is essential.
  • Computational Cost: Outside conjugate families, evaluating the marginal likelihood $\int p(x\mid\theta)p(\theta)\,d\theta$ in high dimensions is intractable, requiring MCMC (NUTS/HMC) or Variational Inference.
  • Model Misspecification: If the likelihood $p(x\mid\theta)$ is misspecified, Bayesian updating still concentrates around the KL-divergence minimizing pseudo-true parameter.
